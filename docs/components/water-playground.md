# Water playground

Last updated: 2026-10-09

[WaterPlayground](../../examples/WaterPlayground/README.md) now contains one full-window, resizable water scene. The former twelve-story selector, inspectors and scene code were removed. [WaterWindow](../classes/WaterWindow.md) owns input and presentation; [WaterSimulation](../classes/WaterSimulation.md) owns the application-side liquid and its coupling to rigid toys. The example consumes only public Electron2D API.

## Physical model

The fixed 1152×800 container receives 65,536 equal-mass particles. One hundred scene units represent a metre and the modeled slice is 0.1 m deep, giving 3.072 m³ at a bulk density of 1,000 kg/m³. Positions, velocities and mass are actual simulation state. The renderer follows those positions; there is no scripted waterline or floating trajectory.

The liquid uses an iterative density-constraint method based on [Position Based Fluids](https://mmacklin.com/pbf_sig_preprint.pdf). Each interval predicts motion under gravity, finds local neighbors, estimates density with a normalized two-dimensional poly6 kernel, solves nonnegative compression using a spiky gradient, projects contacts, reconstructs velocity and applies XSPH viscosity. The numerical profile uses four density iterations, four coupling intervals per 1/60 s tick, 0.25 Jacobi relaxation and 0.1 viscosity. Mirrored wall neighbors supply density support near the container. This finite-iteration real-time model approximates incompressibility and dissipates energy; it is not an exact CFD solution and does not implement air, surface tension or vorticity confinement.

The retained dense neighbor grid covers the basin and prepared air above it. When new or rising particles approach its upper extent, that range grows with headroom; CPU state and GPU buffers preserve existing positions and velocities. The coarse wet field follows the expanded range. Fluid contacts project against the side and bottom boundaries only. There is no upper collider, mirrored ceiling, deletion or respawn: ejected particles continue simulating and can fall back. Particle count remains constant. CPU and GPU implement the same passes and parameters. GPU storage stays resident throughout projection iterations; one state read publishes all 65,536 positions/velocities per outer tick. Small reductions return reaction impulses at each coupling interval. CPU mode uses retained arrays with parallel particle passes; the modes are numerically comparable, not bitwise identical.

The duck, boat and six fish are public PhysicsServer bodies with capsule/circle, convex-hull and capsule geometry. Gravity, rigid-body contacts, mass and inertia use the existing CPU space in both modes. Fluid contacts exchange linear/angular impulses with these bodies before advancing that space. Thus GPU mode identifies the liquid solver; it does not claim that the still-incomplete independent rigid-body GPU backend is exposed. The separate [GPU Smash developer preview](gpu-smash-preview.md) remains test-only and independent of this example.

## Interaction, rendering and lifetime

Water pours from a narrow moving outlet above the visible top edge over eight simulation seconds. The pool reserves 65,536 particles; only released particles take part in queries, constraints and rendering. The duck is inserted at nine seconds, the boat at eleven. Six fish fall in from thirteen seconds onward at 0.3-second intervals. Each complete silhouette starts above the visible upper edge, including in tall windows; its spawn position accounts for the view entry plane and its own bounds. Particle occupancy enables bounded underwater swimming impulses; a grabbed fish stops steering and follows the same physical mouse spring. Out of water, gravity makes it fall. A point spring with gravity compensation and a sufficiently high bounded force lifts toys and fish, including a grab on the sail. Empty-water dragging applies a local force. Release or focus loss ends interaction. Space pauses; R refills; CPU/GPU buttons or Tab preserve released particles and sequence time.

The physical basin is always 1152×800 scene units, independently of window size, fullscreen state and rendering backend. One uniform scale fits the world width to the client width and anchors the world bottom to the client bottom. It preserves object proportions with no side gutters. Aspect-ratio changes expose more or less upper air; input uses the inverse transform. The entire rotated sprite bounds, including tails, beaks and sails, remain within the side and bottom walls after every coupling step. The top is open for water and rigid bodies. Pointer targets clamp sideways and at the bottom but may extend above the window. Resizing never moves existing bodies or particles, or changes mass. It updates only the presentation and the entry plane for future water and body spawns. The spatial grid grows as needed when those new positions lie beyond its prepared range. F11 toggles ordinary borderless fullscreen and restores the prior window size. The example requests a maximum of 144 rendered frames per second and disabled vertical synchronization. On Linux Wayland sessions with an available X display it prefers XWayland for immediate presentation, while respecting an explicit SDL_VIDEO_DRIVER choice. The selected policy is shown in the window; unsupported native modes report the enabled fallback. No system or desktop configuration is modified.

[WaterSurface](../classes/WaterSurface.md) splats solved particles into a retained four-unit density grid spanning the visible vertical range (with filter padding), smooths it, and clips triangles to the density contour. Full interior runs merge into strips. This replaces the old 65,536-disc draw with a compact mesh, with a sharply clipped silhouette and transparent depth tint. Opacity no longer fades with edge density, eliminating the wide blurred fringe. It uses public CanvasItem triangle submission and no per-frame image upload. The water is drawn after toys and six fish; immersed toy silhouettes receive the foreground water tint. Fish are visible both during their fall and underwater; they participate in the same fluid-to-body impulse exchange as the toys.

The fixed step allows one physics interval per display frame so slow CPU work does not accumulate catch-up steps. Simulation time may lag wall time under overload. The window retains its compute device across refills and releases its own simulation resources on disposal.

## Verification and measurements

```sh
ELECTRON2D_TEST_RENDERING_DEVICE=1 dotnet run --project tests/Electron2D.Tests -c Release
ELECTRON2D_TEST_WATER_PLAYGROUND=1 dotnet run --project tests/Electron2D.Tests -c Release
ELECTRON2D_TEST_WATER_PLAYGROUND_NATIVE=1 dotnet run --project tests/Electron2D.Tests -c Release
```

The focused simulation check exercises the full 65,536 particles for 20 simulated seconds, insertion order, side/bottom containment of particles and bodies, offscreen birth of complete body silhouettes, upward fish dragging and falling/swimming fish, settled height, both floating toys, state-preserving CPU/GPU changes, mouse spring behavior and a fixed physical world. A small deterministic initial-state probe launches a droplet from below the former ceiling, verifies upward crossing and return under gravity in both backends, and separately exercises births above the initial grid range. It reports a short sequential settled-state CPU/GPU comparison using identical numerical settings. These are owner-thread full-step timings including rigid coupling, waits and GPU readback, not device timestamp measurements. CPU mode is substantially slower at this fixed population. Prepared engine solver-storage regressions survive in PhysicsSolverStorageTests.

The 2026-10-09 containment/fish check measured 318.389 ms per CPU step and 11.200 ms per GPU step on the local Ryzen 7 5700X/RTX 3090 Ti. These are sequential short samples of the same settled 65,536-particle, eight-body workload. An intermediate two-unit surface mesh with separately drawn contour segments fell to 21–44 FPS and was discarded. The retained four-unit mesh with sharp clipped color recovered roughly 72–125 FPS during native XWayland runs, including wide-window resize. With open-top inflow and the taller visible surface, a later portrait run at 480×800 observed roughly 57–97 FPS during the pour and body drops; the default-width run observed roughly 77–124 FPS. These are short observations, not a stable 144 FPS guarantee or an allocation measurement for the entire scene.

The native check uses the ordinary Engine.Run lifecycle, saves falling-water/toy/resized PNGs under ignored `bin/water-playground/`, and injects mouse/key events for dragging both toys and a fish, CPU/GPU buttons, pause, refill, F11 round-trip, wide/portrait/compact undistorted views, offscreen spawning, fixed existing world coordinates and six submerged fish. Native captures exercise the triangle surface on both rendering backends. ELECTRON2D_WATER_PORTRAIT=1 starts the native check at 480×800 to exercise offscreen birth and rendering of the falling stream in extra visible upper air. Resizing while paused also rebuilds that surface without stepping physics. Linux x64/XWayland/.NET 10 and the local NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 Ti are the exercised profile; other platforms and owner visual acceptance remain separate. The [retired sandbox performance report](physics-sandbox-performance.md) does not describe this liquid scene.
